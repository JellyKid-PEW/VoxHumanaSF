# Wild Huntress — Vertical Stack / Hull Envelope Study

Status: **WORKING GEOMETRY STUDY — NOT A LOCKED EXTERIOR DESIGN**

This study tests whether the currently modeled main and lower decks can occupy one believable commercial long-range utility hull without forcing the exterior to trace every room, corridor, or refit.

## What the current two-deck stack says

### Floor-to-floor spacing

The original 2.7 m main-to-lower floor spacing was too tight for the current lower-deck tall volumes.

Roomwright now uses **3.0 m floor-to-floor** between the main deck and lower deck.

This gives, under occupied main-deck floor:
- approximately 0.45 m above the 2.55 m skiff / mission bay;
- approximately 0.40 m above the 2.60 m configurable mission / flex bay;
- more generous service / structural depth above ordinary lower corridors and cabins.

This interdeck depth is useful ship volume rather than dead space: framing, thermal / environmental trunks, cableways, local distribution, insulation, pressure separation, and maintenance access can occupy it.

The aft main cargo bay is different. It extends farther aft than the modeled main deck and may rise above the nominal main-deck floor plane because there is no full occupied main deck directly above that part of the hull.

### Vertical alignments that work

**Primary stair**
- main-deck stair opening and lower Operations landing align;
- the stair remains a direct command / domestic-to-work-deck connection.

**Secondary domestic ladder**
- the lower residential wet-service core and the main-deck dry hygiene / service vestibule align at the same x/z position;
- this creates a real vertical domestic-service stack rather than two unrelated conveniences.

**Domestic wet zone**
- the main-deck galley / wet-service / hygiene cluster sits generally above the lower residential wet-service / domestic-stores region;
- the main wet-service chase does not need to land exactly on every lower manifold. Short horizontal service runs inside interdeck volume are expected.

### Vertical relationships that should remain distributed

The main-deck walk-in engine bay does **not** need to sit directly above the principal lower engineering hatch.

The Huntress follows the locked distributed-maintenance principle:
- frequent service points appear where systems are used;
- deeper machinery and utility routes connect through layered crawl / service geography;
- not every engineering access is a vertical shaft to one centralized machinery room.

The current arrangement therefore allows the main engine bay, lower engineering recess, lower-aft machinery hatches, and future Deck Three service spaces to participate in one network without being stacked like floors of an office building.

## Hull-envelope implications

The exterior should **not** shrink-wrap itself around each room.

The current internal plan is intentionally asymmetric:
- the main deck's older observation / storage / machinery geography reaches farther starboard;
- the lower residential side reaches farther port;
- the aft lower deck carries the large commercial cargo volume beyond the end of the full main-deck habitation footprint.

A believable hull can smooth over those differences with:
- structural depth;
- tankage;
- cable / duct / coolant trunks;
- insulation and impact protection;
- pressure-shell curvature;
- service voids;
- landing / docking structure;
- local cargo-handling machinery;
- low-frequency reserve volume.

This is desirable. Interior asymmetry can exist inside a comparatively coherent exterior shell.

## Working envelope — visual test only

A useful first exterior test is roughly:
- **interior modeled length:** about 32 m from the forward bridge glass to the aft end of current lower-deck service / cargo geometry;
- **candidate overall hull length:** approximately **40–45 m** after allowing for nose structure, pressure-shell taper, aft propulsion / heat-rejection structure, and service margins;
- **candidate maximum exterior beam:** approximately **16–18 m** if the exterior is kept broadly coherent rather than tightly wrapping the most extreme room at every longitudinal station;
- **occupied two-deck vertical stack:** roughly 5.3 m from lower-deck floor to main-deck overhead before hull structure;
- a future **partial Deck Three** can extend the belly downward locally without requiring a full third inhabited floor.

These dimensions are **not canon**. They are a sanity envelope for deciding whether the interior architecture is forcing a bad ship shape.

## Exterior-shape direction suggested by the interior

The current layout naturally supports a hull with:
1. a relatively compact forward command / entry body around the bridge;
2. a broader mid-body where the main-deck domestic spaces, legacy starboard service geography, lower mission bay, and residential leg overlap;
3. a deeper / fuller aft commercial belly containing the large cargo volume and lower-aft service geography;
4. upper-deck inhabited volume tapering out before the full aft cargo body ends;
5. propulsion / heat-rejection structure continuing aft beyond the currently modeled rooms.

This would make the Huntress look like a **working utility transport**, not a smooth passenger yacht and not a rectangular three-story building.

## Deck Three

Deck Three remains intentionally unresolved.

The current stack supports the locked concept:
- **partial lower service / reserve structure**, not a full-footprint basement;
- walkable in some regions, crawl / machinery / tank volume in others;
- reserve storage and low-frequency service access where geometry supports it.

Do not create a full third-floor rectangle merely because the ship has a "Deck Three" reference.

## Next exterior-study questions

Before locking any hull silhouette:
- decide how much of the aft cargo volume rises into the notional main-deck plane;
- decide where propulsion / drive structure begins relative to the current walk-in engine bay and aft cargo body;
- test whether a 16–18 m maximum beam feels appropriately substantial rather than barge-like;
- decide whether the bridge sits near the exterior centerline or is modestly offset inside a broader hull;
- determine where the observation cupola actually breaks the outer shell and how its protective shutter integrates with that shell.

The current interior does **not** require an absurd exterior. It does require the exterior to be treated as a hull with real structural / service volume rather than as a skin wrapped tightly around room rectangles.
